Supervised Learning

The matrix formulation of both feed-foward neural networks and convolutional neural networks (and other networks as well, for example, transformers) is inspired by the linear relationship between the parameters of the network. However, when the dimensions of the space the parameters live in begins to grow, and so do the relationships between parameters, matrices become an inappropriate tool to mathematically describe the model and a rather clumsy tool to describe the backpropogation algorithm, so here, tensors are chosen instead. Tensors arive when the sample space needs multiple numbers to index the basis, such as in the case of image data. The first part of the article deals with classical feed-forward networks, where the data is assumed to be linearized, so tensors won't make their appearance there. The second section however deals with convolutional nets, which do deal with higher dimensional data. Tensors aren't technically required to formally talk about such data; all one needs really is a multiindex-able object, say something like $\reals^{a \times b \times \ldots}$. Infact these can be identified with tensors. Tensors are chosen because the machinery they come with makes alot of the derivations much cleaner.

1. Feed Forward Neural Networks

We will call a neural network a layered model that produces features of samples in a "linear" way, by using parameters and the sample features at previous layers and then applying a non-linear transform. Define the following:
  • Dataset: $$\mathcal{D} = \{\{{\bar{x}_{(i)}}^{j}\}_{j \leq J}, \bar{v}_{(i)}\}_{i \leq I} $$ for some $I \in \Bbb{N}$ where ${x_{(i)}}^{j} \in \mathbb{R}$ is the jth feature of sample $i$ and $v_{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}$ is its label
  • The $j$th feature of the $i$th sample produced at hidden layer $l-1$, feeding into hidden layer $l$. $${y_{(i,l-1)}^{j}$$
Suppose now we have ${y_{(i,l-1)}}^{j}$ for every $j \leq J_l$. We aim to produce [one of] the next features ${y_{(i, l)}}^{k}$ for the same sample $i$; namely feature number $k \leq K_l=J_{l+1}$ not necessarily equal to $j$. The feed-forward neural network (FFN) achieves this in two steps:
  1. We take a set of parameters $\{W_{(l)}^{j,k}, b_{(l)}^k \in \Bbb{R}\}_{j \leq J_l, k \leq K_l=J_{l+1}}$ - we may also call these weights and biases - and multiply each to the respective $j$th feature of $y_{(i, l-1)}$ at layer $l-1$, before summing. That is, we produce $$ \begin{equation} (z_{(i,l)})^{k} = \sum_{j \leq J_l}y_{(i, l-1)}^{j} W_{(l)}^{j,k} + b_{(l)}^k \end{equation} $$ a "preimage" of the $k$th feature of the $i$th sample at layer $l$. Just the preimage thus far, since at step 2...
  2. a smooth, nonlinear transform from $C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R})$ is henceforth applied: $$ \begin{equation} (y_{(i,l)})^{k} = f_l((z_{(i,l)})^{k}) \end{equation} $$ producing the desired feature.

Tensors

It is at this point we will specify important details needed to understand the rest of the article. Firstly, we will use Einstein's Summation Convention (ESC) to simplify sums with a repeated index, signifying the summation of the terms over said index. For example, we can rewrite (1) as $$ \begin{equation} (z_{(i,l)})^{k} = (y_{(i,l-1)})^{j} W_{(l)}^{j,k} + b^k \end{equation} $$ where the sum is meant to be over $j$, which repeats, and where all terms are zero outside of their domains. This too deserves formalization. Firstly, let's consider $(y_{(i,l)})$ as a well defined object, namely, a tensor over infinite dimensional vector spaces, but with finite support. Let's clarify, fix a feature tensor at layer $l$: $$y_{(i,l)} = (y_{(i,l)})^{j} e_j \in \Bbb{V}$$ Importantly, this is just a vector. However we are going to blur the distinction to warm up he reader to tensors, since this can be considered a tensor with a single slot. Let's also define what samples are $$ x_{(i)} = x_{(i)}^j e_j \in \Bbb{V} $$ again, using ESC. Now we can define the other objects we'll use $$ \begin{align} \bar{\bm{x}} &= \prod_i {\bar{x}_{(i)}}^{j} e_j &&\in \prod_i \Bbb{V} \\ \bar{\bm{v}} &= \sum_i \bar{v}_{(i)} e_i &&\in \mathbb{R}^{I}\\ W_{(l)} &= W_{(l)}^{j,k} e_j \otimes e_k &&\in \Bbb{V} \otimes \Bbb{V} \\ b_{(l)} &= b_{(l)}^{k} e_k &&\in \Bbb{V} \end{align} $$
Throughout the article, $\mathbb{V} = \mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}}$ and $\mathbb{V}^{\otimes 2} = \mathbb{V} \otimes \mathbb{V} $
We also need to transform the $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ transform into a $\Bbb{V}\to \Bbb{V}$ transform that acts on tensors, by defining $$ \begin{equation} y_{(i,l)} = F_l(z_{(i,l)}) = f_l({z_{(i,l)}}^{k}) e_k \end{equation} $$ We will also make use of Abstract Index Notation (AIN), which is particularly useful when representing the shape of a tensor. For example, we could write $$ T^{ab}{}_{c}{}^{d}{}_{e} $$ to denote a tensor $T \in V \otimes V \otimes V^* \otimes V \otimes V^*$.

Contraction, Inner Products, Metric Tensors


Using basic linear algebra, one realizes that $z_{(i,l)}$ can be formulated as a matrix product, where the inner product is over the space indexed by $j$. Using tensors however, we see it as the image of the metric tensor, or, if one of the $j$ were lowered, as a contraction. Before jumping in, we need to agree on contraction notation; in (AIN) this is straighforward:
Let $S^{a}{}_{b} = S^i{}_j e_i \otimes e^j $ be arbitrary. Contraction along slots $a,b$ is defined as $$ S^{a}{}_{a} = S^i{}_j e^j(e_i) $$ In the case where $e^j(e_i) = \delta_{ij}$: $$ S^{a}{}_{b} = S^i{}_i $$ which using ESC is just the sum of the diagonal indices.
We should talk briefly about the metric tensor $g_{ab}$ and the inverse metric tensor $ {g}^{ab}$. $\Bbb{V}$ has the canonical orthonormal basis $\{e_i \}$, and is endowed with the canonical inner product $\langle e_i , e_j \rangle = \delta_{ij}$. Such an inner product admits a dual basis for $\Bbb{V}^*$ defined by $\{e^i = \langle \cdot, e_i \rangle\} $. The metric tensors are then given by agreement with the inner products $$\begin{align} g_{ab} = g_{i,j} e^i \otimes e^j = \delta_{ij} e^i \otimes e^j \\ {g}^{ab} = g^{i,j} e_i \otimes e_j = \delta_{ij} e_i \otimes e_j \end{align}$$ Now we can now show contraction against the metric $g$
$$ g_{ab} S^{a} T^{b} = \delta_{ij} S^p e^i(e_p) T^q e^j(e_q) = S^i T^i $$ $$ g^{ab} S_{a} T_{b} = \delta_{ij} S_p e^p(e_i) T_q e^q(e_j) = S_i T_i $$ A [very] useful property is that the metric tensors can raise/lower indices. For example, $$ g_{ab} v^a = \delta_{ij} e^i \otimes e^j(v^p e_p) = v_{(i)} e^i = v_a $$ It should be understood that if $v_a$ is given when $v^a$ was defined, the index has been moved via application of a metric tensor.
Now we can find a suitable tensorial representation for $z_{(i,l)}$:
$$ z_{(i,l)} = (y^{(i,l-1)})_{a}W_{(l)}^{ab} + b_{(l)} $$

The Model

We now formalize the FNN model $\mathcal{F}$ completely.

  • We will call a layer $l=1...L$ the function $$y_{(i,l-1)} \mapsto \mathcal{L}_l(y_{(i,l-1)} ; W_{(l)}, b_{(l)}) = F_l \circ \Gamma_l(y_{(i,l-1)}, W_{(l)}, b_{(l)})$$ where $$ \begin{equation} \Gamma_{l}(S^a,T^{ab},U^{a}) = S_{a}T^{ab}+ U^a \end{equation} $$ We will call the neuron $k$ at layer $l$ the function $$ y_{(i,l-1)} \mapsto \mathcal{N}_{l,k}(y_{(i,l-1)} ; W_{(l)}^{j,k} e_j , b_{(l)}^k) = F_l \circ \gamma_{l}(y_{(i,l-1)}, W_{(l)}^{j,k} e_j, b_{(l)}^k) $$ where $$ \gamma_{l}(S^a,T^a, u) = S_{a}T^{a}+ u $$ Note that $$ \mathcal{N}_{l,k}(y_{(i,l-1)} ; W_{(l)}^{j,k} e_j , b_{(l)}^k) e_k = \mathcal{L}_l(y_{(i,l-1)}; W_{(l)}, b_{(l)}) $$ that is, a layer is a sum of neurons.
  • First we start with the input layer , $\mathcal{L}_0$. We define $$y_{(i,0)} := x_{(i)} $$ for any sample $x_{(i)} \in \Bbb{V}$ and define $\mathcal{L}_0$ by $x_{(i)} \mapsto y_{(i,0)}$, so $\mathcal{L}_0$ is the identity. It's not strictly a computational layer like defined earlier, however some folks like to speak of an input layer, which refers to this.
  • The hidden layers are $\{\mathcal{L}_l\}_{ l=1\dots L}$
  • The output layer is $$y_{(i,L)} \mapsto \mathcal{L}_{L+1}(y_{(i,L)}; W_{(L+1)}, b_{(L+1)}) = F_{L+1} \circ \Gamma(y_{(i,L)}, W_{(L+1)}, b_{(L+1)})$$ where $F_{L+1}$ is called the activation function.
  • The model $\mathcal{F}$ is defined by $$ \mathcal{F}(x ; \theta) = \mathcal{L}_{L+1}(\cdot ; W_{(L+1)},b_{(L+1)}) \circ \ldots \circ \mathcal{L}_{1}(\cdot ; W_{(1)}, b_{(1)} \circ \mathcal{L_0} (x) $$ where $$\theta = W_{(1)},b_{(1)} \ldots W_{(L+1)},b_{(L+1)} $$ are the parameters of the model.

Loss, Empirical Risk

We have so far the ability to compute the output of our parameterized model $y_{(i,L+1)} = \mathcal{F}(x_{(i)};\theta)$.

Let $X,Y$ be the random variables for input features and labels, over the spaces $\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{Y} $. Let $$X,Y \sim \mathcal{P}(X,Y) $$ with pdf $p_{X,Y}(x,y)$. Let $X_i,Y_i$ be the ith feature and label random variables, and assume $$X_i, Y_i \sim_{i.i.d} \mathcal{P}(X,Y)$$ Define a loss function $\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{V} \to \mathbb{R}:$ $$ w, \hat{w} \mapsto \Delta(w, \hat{w}) $$ The loss at a point $(x,y) \in \mathbb{V} \times \mathbb{R}$ is $$ l(x, y,h) = \Delta(y, h(x)) $$ The risk is the expected value of the loss over $\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}$ $$ R(h) = \mathbb{E}_{X,Y}(l(x,y,h)) = \int l(x,y,h) p_{X,Y}(x,y) dxdy $$ which is intractable, so the empirical risk over a sample $\bm{d}=( \bm{x}, \bm{y})$ is used instead, which, as data size grows, converges to the risk by the LLN: $$ \begin{equation} \hat{R}(\bm{d}, h) = \frac{1}{I}\sum_i l(x_i,y_i,h) \to \mathbb{E}_{X,Y}(l(x,y,h)) \end{equation} $$as $I\to\infty$. Empirical Risk Minimization chooses a model space $\mathcal{H}$ and finds $$ \argmin_{h \in \mathcal{H}}\hat{R}(\bm{d}, h)$$
For our purposes, $\mathcal{X}=\Bbb{V}, \mathcal{Y}=\Bbb{R}$. Our loss is defined as $$ l(x_{(i)}, v_{(i)}, \mathcal{F}(\cdot; \theta)) = \Delta(v, \mathcal{F}(x_{(i)};\theta)) $$ and for a sample $\bm{d} = (\bm{x}, \bm{v})$ $$ \hat{R}(\bm{d}, \mathcal{F}(\cdot; \theta)) = \frac{1}{I}\sum_i l(x_{(i)}, v_{(i)}, \mathcal{F}(\cdot; \theta)) \to \Bbb{E}_{X, Y}(l(x, v, \mathcal{F}(\cdot; \theta))) $$
So given our sample $\bar{\bm{d}} = (\bar{\bm{x}}, \bar{\bm{v}})$ we find $$ \argmin_{\theta} \hat{R}(\bar{\bm{d}}, \mathcal{F}(\cdot ; \theta)) = \argmin_{\theta}\frac{1}{I}\sum_i l(x_{(i)},\bar{v}_{(i)}, \mathcal{F}(\cdot; \theta)) \\ = \argmin_{\theta}\frac{1}{I}\sum_i \Delta(\bar{v}_{(i)}, \mathcal{F}(\bar{x}_{(i)}; \theta)) $$

Loss Function, Activation Functions

We will need a way to choose the loss function $\Delta$. While we could make an arbitrary choice, we could also use Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) to derive it. Let $$\mathscr{P}=\{\mathcal{P}(X,Y; \theta) : p_{X,Y}(x,y;\theta) = p_{Y|X}(y|x;\theta)p_X(x) = p_0(y;\mathcal{F}(x;\theta))p_X(x) \}_{\theta \in \Theta }$$ and assume $$X,Y \sim \mathcal{P}(X, Y; {\theta}) \in \mathscr{P} $$ with density $p_{X,Y}(x,y;\theta) = p_0(y;\mathcal{F}(x;\theta))p_X(x)$ for some $\theta$. The sample distribution for the random vector ${\bf D}= ({\bf X}, {\bf Y})$ is then given by the density $$ p_{{\bf D}}(\bm{d};\theta)=_{i.i.d} \prod_i p(x_{(i)}, y_{(i)};\theta) = \prod_i p_0(y_{(i)};\mathcal{F}(x_{(i)};\theta))p_X(x_{(i)})$$
With our $\mathcal{X} = \Bbb{V}, \mathcal{Y} = \Bbb{R}$ and $\bm{d} = \bm{x}, \bm{v}$ $$ p_{\bf D} (\bm{d}; \theta)= \prod_i p_0(v_{(i)};\mathcal{F}(x_{(i)};\theta))p_X(x_{(i)}) $$
The likelihood is $$ \mathbb{L}(\theta; \bar{\bm{d}}) = p_{\bf D}(\bar{\bm{d}}; \theta) = \prod_i p_0(\bar{v}_{(i)};\mathcal{F}(\bar{x}_{(i)};\theta))p_X(\bar{x}_{(i)}) $$ Taking the negative log gives $$ -\log(\mathbb{L}(\theta; \bm{\bar{d}})) = -\sum_i \log(p_X(\bar{x}_{(i)})) - \sum_i \log(p_0(\bar{v}_{(i)};\mathcal{F}(\bar{x}_{(i)}; \theta))) $$ which we want minimized over $\theta$. To do so, we need only find $$ \begin{equation} \argmin_{\theta}\left(- \sum_i \log(p_0(\bar{v}_{(i)};\mathcal{F}(\bar{x}_{(i)}; \theta)))\right) \end{equation} $$ If we set $$\Delta(a, b) := -\log(p_0(a;b)) $$ we then have $$ \argmin_{\theta} (- \log(\Bbb{L}(\theta; \bar{\bm{d}})) = \argmin_{\theta}\left(\sum_i \Delta(\bar{v}_{(i)};\mathcal{F}(\bar{x}_{(i)}; \theta))\right) =\argmin_{\theta} \hat{R}(\bar{\bm{d}},\mathcal{F}(\cdot; \theta))$$ so maximizing the likelihood assuming class $\mathscr{P}$ will also minimize the empirical risk with this loss. By our assumption on the data and $\mathscr{P}$, $$(Y|X=x) \sim \mathcal{P}(Y|X=x; \theta) $$ with $$p_{Y|X=x}(y|x;\theta)=p_0(y;\mathcal{F}(x;\theta))$$ $$\implies (Y|X=x) \sim \mathcal{P}_0(Y;\mathcal{F}(x;\theta))$$ for some apriori chosen $\mathcal{P}_0$ with density $p_0$. We now investigate suitable choices for $\mathcal{P}_0$ by the nature of the random variable $(Y|X=x)$:
  • If $(Y|X=x) \in \{0,1\}$ in the case of a binary learning task, then $$\mathcal{P}_0(Y;\hat{y}) = \textbf{Bernoulli}(Y;\hat{y})$$ where $\hat{y} = \mathcal{F}(x;\theta)$, is a suitable choice (although actually derived from the axioms of probability) with $$p_0(y;\hat{y}) = \hat{y}^y(1-\hat{y})^{1-y} $$ $$\implies \Delta(v, \hat{y})= -\log(p_0(v, \hat{y})) = -\left(v\log(\hat{y}) + (1-v)\log(1-\hat{y})\right) $$ that is, the Binary Cross Entropy (BCE) loss. Such a loss demands
    1. $\hat{ y} \in \reals$
    2. $ \hat{y} \in [0,1]$
    To satisfy (a), $$ W_{(L+1)} \in \mathbb{V} $$ is required. To satisfy b), we choose the activation $f_{L+1}$ to be the sigmoid activation $$ f_{L+1}: \reals \to \reals = z \mapsto \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} $$
  • If $(Y|X=x) \in S \subset \mathbb{Z} : \lvert S \rvert < \infty$ more broadly, in the case of categorical learning, then $$ \mathcal{P}_0(Y;{\hat{ y}})=\textbf{Categorical}(Y;{\hat{ y}}) $$ where ${\hat{ y}}=\mathcal{F}(x;\theta)$, is a suitable choice (although actually derived from the axioms of probability) with $$p_0(y;{\hat{y}})=\prod_c ( \hat{y}^c)^{[y=c]}$$ $$\implies \Delta(v, {\hat{ y}})=-\log(p_0(v, {\hat{y}}))=-\sum_c [v=c]\log(\hat{y}^c) $$ that is, the Categorical Cross Entropy (CCE) loss. Such a loss demands our output $\hat{ y}=\mathcal{F}(x, \theta)$ obey certain properties:
    1. $ \text{supp}(\hat{y}) \subset \{e_c\}_{c\leq C} (\implies \text{supp}(z_{(L+1)}) \subset \{e_c\}_{c\leq C}) $
    2. $\hat{y}^{c} \in [0,1], \sum_c \hat{y}^{c} = 1$
    so that $\Delta$ is well defined. To satisfy (b) we choose the activation $F_{L+1}$ to be the softmax activation, a type of tensor activation: $$ F_{L+1}: \mathbb{V} \to \mathbb{V} = z_{(i,L+1)}^{j}e_j \mapsto \frac{e^{z_{(i,L+1)}^{j}}}{\sum_k e^{z_{(i,L+1)}^{k}}} e_j $$ At layer $L+1$, there is only one neuron, given by $$\mathcal{N}_{L+1, 1} e_1 = \mathcal{L}_{L+1}$$ with $$\text{supp}^b ((W_{(L+1)})^{ab}) \subset \{e_c\}_{c\leq C} $$ so that $(a)$ is satisfied.
  • If $(Y|X=x) \in \mathbb{R}$ in the case of a regression, then $$ \mathcal{P}_0(Y;\hat{y})=\mathcal{N}(Y;\mu=\hat{y},\sigma^2=1) $$ where $\hat{y} = \mathcal{F}(x;\theta)$, is a suitable choice with $$p_0(y;\hat{y})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left({-\frac{(y-\hat{y})^2}{2}}\right)$$ $$\implies \Delta(v, \hat{y})= -\log(p_0(v, \hat{y})) =-\log(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}) + \frac{1}{2}(v - \hat{y})^2 $$ although note to minimize the above, one need only minimize the Mean Squared Error (MSE) term $(v-\hat{y})^2$. Such a loss demands our output $\hat{y}=\mathcal{F}(x, \theta)$ obey certain properties:
    1. $\hat{y} \in \reals $
    so that $\Delta$ is well defined. To satisfy this requirement $$ W_{(L+1)} \in \mathbb{V} $$ is required. For regression, the activation is unrestricted and can infact be left out entirely by being set to the identity.

Backpropogation

We can now formulate the backpropogation algorith which will be used to find $$ \theta^* = \argmin_{\theta} \hat{R}(\bar{\bm{d}}, \mathcal{F}(\cdot; \theta)) $$ via Gradient Descent. Notice $$ \argmin_{\theta} \hat{R}(\bar{\bm{d}}, \mathcal{F}(\cdot; \theta)) = \argmin_{\theta} \left(\frac{1}{I}\sum_i \Delta(\bar{v}_{(i)};\mathcal{F}(\bar{x}_{(i)}; \theta))\right) \\ = \argmin_{\theta} \left(\sum_i \Delta(\bar{v}_{(i)};\mathcal{F}(\bar{x}_{(i)}; \theta))\right) = \frac{1}{I} \sum_i \argmin_{\theta} \left( \Delta(\bar{v}_{(i)};\mathcal{F}(\bar{x}_{(i)}; \theta))\right)$$ so we could find the $\argmin$ pointwise then average. In practice, solving for $\theta^* = \argmin \hat{R}(\bar{\bm{d}}; \mathcal{F}(\cdot; \theta))$ is infeasible for many reasons, two important ones being the many flat regions of the objective $\hat{R}$ and the large matrix inversions that would have to be computed. However, if we've obtained the partial derivative of $\hat{R}(\bar{\bm{d}}; \mathcal{F}(\cdot; \theta))$ with respect to $\theta$ we can subtract the gradient from $\theta$ to move towards the optimal point. In practice [again, sorry], it's helpful to vectorize along the batch sample because the partial derivatives will be more computationally efficient to work with. Right now, we would have to find $I$ partials $$ \partial_{\theta} \hat{R}(\bar{\bm{d}}; \mathcal{F}(\cdot; \theta)) = \frac{1}{I} \sum_i \partial_{\theta} \Delta(\bar{v}_{(i)};\mathcal{F}(\bar{x}_{(i)}; \theta)) $$ which on its own is fine, except for the fact that the intermediate backpropogation steps would require looping over each point. Instead, we would rather backpropogate aggregate objects over the entire dataset. To do this, we need to define a secondary model where the entire forward propogation concerns only a single object for all points. Define the vectored version of the loss function$\Delta$ by $$ {\bf \Delta}(\bm{v}, \hat{\bm y}) = \sum_i \Delta(v_{(i)}, \hat{y}_{(i)}) $$ so that $$ {\bf \Delta}(\bm{\bar{v}}, \mathcal{F}(\bar{\bm{x}}; \theta)) = \sum_i \Delta(\bar{v}_{(i)}, \mathcal{F}(\bar{x}_{(i)}; \theta)) \\ \implies \hat{R}(\bar{\bm{d}}, \mathcal{F}(\cdot; \theta)) = \frac{1}{I} {\bf \Delta}(\bm{\bar{v}}, \mathcal{F}(\bar{\bm{x}}; \theta)) $$ Note the overloading of the symbol $\mathcal{F}$, here it is taken to mean $$ \mathcal{F}(\bm{x}; \theta) := \bigoplus_i \mathcal{F}(x_{(i)};\theta) $$ This type of overload will be used for all the other module functions such as $F_l, \Gamma_l \ldots$, but it should be very clear from the type of argument given. For any objects $v_i$, the vectored version can be given as $$ \bm{v} = v_1 \oplus \ldots \oplus v_I = \sum_i e_i \otimes v_i $$ since $$ \reals^I \otimes \Bbb{V} \cong \bigoplus_i \Bbb{V}$$ We define each $\bm{z}_{(l)}, \bm{y}_{(l)}, \Gamma_l, F_l$ similarly $$ \bm{z}_{(l)} = z_{(1,l)} \oplus \ldots \oplus z_{(I, l)} = \sum_i e_i \otimes z_{(i,l)} \\ \bm{y}_{(l)} = y_{(1,l)} \oplus \ldots \oplus y_{(I, l)} = \sum_i e_i \otimes y_{(i,l)} \\ \Gamma_l(\bm{y}_{(l-1)}) := \bigoplus_i \Gamma_l(y_{(i,l-1)};\theta) = \sum_ie_i \otimes \Gamma_l(y_{(i,l-1)};\theta) \\ F_l(\bm{z}_{(l)}) := \bigoplus_i F_l(z_{(i,l)}) = \sum_ie_i \otimes F_l(z_{(i,l)}) $$ and note $\bm{z}_{(l)} = \Gamma_l(\bm{y}_{(l-1)};\theta) $ and $\bm{y}_{(l)} = F_l(\bm{z}_{(l)}) $. So we actually have the same model $$ \mathcal{F}(\bm{x}; \theta) = \bigoplus_i \mathcal{F}(x_i; \theta) = \bm{y}_{(L+1)} = F_{L+1} \circ \ldots \circ \Gamma_1(\cdot; \theta)(\bm{x}) $$ that is, instead of direct summing at the end, we can compose the above maps into a model on batches. Infact, the direct sum satisfies, for any $f(x) = f_n \circ \ldots \circ f_1(x)$ $$ \bigoplus_i f(x_i) = \bigoplus_i f_n \circ \ldots \circ \bigoplus_i f_1 (\bigoplus_i x_i) $$ Define $$ \begin{align*} S(v, z) &= \Delta(v, \cdot) \circ F_{L+1}(z) \\ T(\cdot, \theta) &= \Gamma_{L+1}(\cdot, \theta) \circ ... \circ \Gamma_1(\cdot,\theta) \\ S(\bar{\bm{v}}, \bm{z}_{(L+1)}) &= {\bf \Delta}(\bm{\bar{v}}, \cdot) \circ F_l(\bm{z}_{(L+1)}) \\ &= \sum_i \Delta(\bar{v}_{(i)}, F_{L+1}(z_{(i,L+1)})) \\&= \sum_i S(\bar{v}_{(i)}, z_{(i, L+1)}) \\ T(\bm{x}, \theta) &= \bigoplus_i T(x_{(i)}, \theta) \\&= \Gamma_{L+1}(\cdot,\theta) \circ \ldots \circ \Gamma_1(\cdot , \theta) (\bm{x}) \end{align*} $$ Then $$ \hat{R}(\bar{\bm{d}}, \mathcal{F}(\cdot;\theta)) = \frac{1}{I} \sum_i S(\bar{v}_{(i)}, \cdot) \circ T(\bar{x}_{(i)}, \theta)= \frac{1}{I} S(\bar{\bm{v}}, \cdot) \circ \ T(\bar{\bm{x}}, \theta) $$
$$ \begin{align*} \partial_{\theta} \Delta(\bar{v}_{(i)}, \mathcal{F}(\bar{x}_{(i)}; \theta)) &= \partial_2S (\bar{v}_{(i)}, z_{(i,L+1)}) \circ \partial_{2} T(\bar{x}_{(i)},\theta) \\ \partial_{\theta}\hat{R}(\bar{\bm{d}}, \mathcal{F}(\cdot;\theta)) &= \frac{1}{I} \partial_2 S(\bar{\bm{v}}, \bm{z}_{(L+1)}) \circ \partial_2 T(\bar{\bm{x}}, \theta) \end{align*} $$
Proof
Follows from the chain rule.

It turns out that combining $\Delta$ and $F_{L+1}$ into $S$ results in very elegant derivatives, which we will derive below.
If $\hat y = \mathcal{F}(x, \theta) \in \reals $ and $\Delta(\bar{v}_{(i)}, \hat{y}_{(i)}) = -\left(\bar{v}_{(i)}\log(\hat{y}_{(i)}) + (1-\bar{v}_{(i)})\log(\hat{y}_{(i)})\right) $ in the case of a binary learning task, then $$ \partial_{\theta} \hat{R}(\bar{\bm{d}}, \mathcal{F}(\cdot; \theta)) = \frac{1}{I} \langle \cdot,\bm{\hat{y}} -\bar{\bm{v}} \rangle \circ \partial_{2} T(\bar{\bm{ x}},\theta) $$

The Hom-Tensor Identification

A word on the canonical identification: if we have a map $T \in \text{Hom}(V, W)$ where $V$ is a tensor product space with basis $\{e_{\mathcal{I}}\} $, and the basis for $V^*$ is induced by the standard inner product $\langle e_{\mathcal{I}} , e_{\mathcal{J}} \rangle = \delta_{\mathcal{I}, \mathcal{J}} $ giving $\{(e_{\mathcal{I}})' = \langle \cdot , e_{\mathcal{I}} \rangle \}$, then using $$ \text{Hom}(V,W) \cong W\otimes V^* \\ (\bigotimes V_i)^* \cong \bigotimes V_i^* $$ we have the identification
$$ \phi: T \mapsto T(e_{\mathcal{I}}) \otimes (e_{\mathcal{I}})' \mapsto T(e_{\mathcal{I}}) \otimes e^{\mathcal{I}} = \phi(T) $$

Adjunction

The gradient is technically the adjoint of the derivative $$ \nabla f(x) = (Df(x))^{\dagger} $$ Since we will express all derivatives as tensors, we need to quickly mention the suitable 'adjunction' operator for tensors: if $T \in \text{Hom}(V, W)$ where $V$ has basis $\{e_{\mathcal{I}}\}$, $W$ has basis $\{f_{\mathcal{I}}\}$, and both spaces are endowed with the standard products $\langle e_{\mathcal{I}}, e_{\mathcal{J}}\rangle = \delta_{\mathcal{I}, \mathcal{J}}$ and $\langle f_{\mathcal{I}}, f_{\mathcal{J}}\rangle = \delta_{\mathcal{I}, \mathcal{J}}$ , and $V^*, W^*$ have the dual bases induced from these inner products, then $$ \phi(T^{\dagger}) = \langle f_{\mathcal{J}}, T(e_{\mathcal{I}}) \rangle e_{\mathcal{I}} \otimes f^{\mathcal{J}} $$ Also $$ \phi(T) = T(e_{\mathcal{I}}) \otimes e^{\mathcal{I}} \\ = \langle f_{\mathcal{J}}, T(e_{\mathcal{I}}) \rangle f_{\mathcal{J}} \otimes e^{\mathcal{I}} $$ Thus 'adunction' on tensors can be defined by
$$ \phi(T^a{}_b)^{\dagger} := T^b{}_{a} = \phi(T^{\dagger}) $$

2. Convolutional Neural Networks

The machinery used to formalize FNNs is even more applicable to convolutional neural networks (CNNs). Lets start with the key operation; define the operator $$ Y^{bcd} *^{\pm} W^{abcd} = \sum_{s,r}Y_{bcd} \tau(W; \pm i, \pm j)^{abcd} \otimes e_{s} \otimes e_r $$ where $$ \tau(W;s,r) = W^{i,j,k-s,l-r} e_{i,j,k,l} = W^{i,j,k,l} e_{i,j,k+s,l+r} $$ is the translation operator. Now let $$\chi(W)^{i,j,k,l} = W^{i,j,-k,-l}$$ be the tensor flipped at the last $2$ slots. Define a few operators $$ \begin{align*} &Y \rtimes W = Y *^+ W \\ &Y \bar{\rtimes} W = Y *^+ \chi(W) \\ &Y \ltimes W = Y *^- W \\ &Y \bar{\ltimes} W = Y *^- \chi(W) \end{align*} $$ Let $y_{(l-1)}^c, W_{l}^c \in \Bbb{V}^{\otimes 4}, b_{l}^c \in \Bbb{V}$.

The Model

The convolutional layer is defined for $l=1\ldots L_c$ as the map $$ y_{(i,l-1)}^c \mapsto \mathcal{L}^c_l(y_{(i,l-1)}^c;W_{(l)}^c, b_{(l)}^c) = F^c_l \circ \Gamma^c_l(y_{(i,l-1)}^c, W_{(l)}^c, b_{(l)}^c) $$ where $$ \Gamma^c_l(Y, W, b) =Y \rtimes W + b \otimes \bm{1}^{ab} $$ We will call the neuron $k$ at layer $l$ the function $$ y_{(i,l-1)}^c \mapsto \mathcal{N}^c_{l,k}(y_{(i,l-1)}^c;(W_{(l)}^c)^{k,m,i,j}e_{m,i,j}, (b_{(l)}^c)^{k}) = F^c_l \circ \Gamma^c_l(y_{(i,l-1)}^c, (W_{(l)}^c)^{k,m,i,j} e_{m,i,j}, (b_{(l)}^c)^{k}) $$ Note that $$ e_k \otimes\mathcal{N}^c_{l,k}(y_{(l-1)}^c;(W_{(l)}^c)^{k,m,i,j}e_{m,i,j}, (b_{(l)}^c)^{k}) = \mathcal{L}^c_l(y_{(l-1)}^c;W_{(l)}^c, b_{(l)}^c)$$ The flattening layer changes the dimension of $y^c_{(i,L_c)}$ so that $y_{(i,0)} = y^c_{(i,L_c+1)} \in \Bbb{V}$ as follows: Let $T \in \Bbb{V}^{\otimes n}$ for some $n$. Define $$ \alpha_{m} = \min \{ \alpha | e_{\alpha} \in \text{supp}(T) \} \\ \alpha_{M} = \max \{ \alpha | e_{\alpha} \in \text{supp}(T) \} \\ d_i = (\alpha_M)_i - (\alpha_m)_i + 1$$ for $i=1\ldots n$, and $\beta = (\beta_1 \ldots \beta_n)$ such that $$ \beta_j = \begin{cases} \prod_{i=j+1}^n d_i & i < n \\ 1 & i = n \end{cases} $$ Let $|\alpha| = \sum_i \alpha_i$. The Gradients

Define the following: $$ \begin{align*} {\bm{\xi}}_l{} &= \phi(\partial_{1} U^c_l{}(\bm{y}^c_{(l)}{}, \theta)) &= ({\xi}^c_l{})_{tuvw} e^{tuvw}\\ {\bm{\xi}}_z &= \phi(\partial_{1} U^c_l{}(\bm{y}^c_{(l)}{}, \theta) \circ DF^c_l{}(\bm{z}_{(l)}^c)) \\ {\bm{\delta}}_w &= \phi(\partial_{W^c_{(l)}{}} \hat{R}(\bar{\bm{d}}; \mathcal{F}(\cdot; \theta))) \\ {\bm{\delta}}_b &= \phi(\partial_{b^c_l{}} \hat{R}(\bar{\bm{d}}; \mathcal{F}(\cdot; \theta)))\\ \bm{\epsilon}_w &= \phi(\partial_{W^c_{(l)}{}}\Gamma^c_l{}(\bm{y}_{(l-1)}, \theta^c_l{})) &= (y^c_{(l-1)})^{i\beta(\gamma+s)(\delta+r)} e_{i\alpha sr} \otimes e^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\\ \bm{\epsilon}_b &= \phi(\partial_{b^c_l{}}\Gamma^c_l{}(\bm{y}_{(l-1)}, \theta^c_l{})) &= \bm{1}^{a} \otimes e_{\alpha} \otimes \bm{1}^{bc} \otimes e^{\alpha} \\ \bm{\epsilon}_z &= \phi(DF^c_l{}({\bm{z}}^c_{(l)}{})) & = f^{c}_l{}'((z^c_{(l)})^{ijmn}) \otimes e_{ijmn} \otimes e^{ijmn} \\ \bm{\epsilon}_{l-1} &= \phi(\partial_{1} \Gamma^c_l{}(\bm{y}_{(l-1)}; \theta^c_{(l)}) &= {W^c_{(l)}}^{j\beta (\gamma-s)(\delta-r)} e_{\alpha jsr} \otimes e^{\alpha \beta \gamma \delta} \\ \end{align*} $$ By the preceding work we can find the gradient tensors for each parameter. NOTE: all transpositions denote permuting the first two slots.
$$ \bm{\delta}_w^{\dagger} = (\bm{y}^c_{(l-1)}{}^T \rtimes \bm{\xi}^T_z)^T $$
Proof
$$ \begin{align*} \bm{\delta}_w &= \phi \left( \partial_{1} U^c_l{}(\bm{y}^c_{(l)}{}, \theta) \circ DF^c_l{}({\bm{z}}^c_{(l)}{}) \circ \partial_{W^c_{(l)}{}}\Gamma^c_l{}(\bm{y}_{(l-1)}, \theta^c_{(l)}) \right) \\ &= (\bm{\xi}_z){}_{abcd} (\bm{\epsilon}_w)^{abcd}{}_{efgh} \\ &= (\xi_z)_{tuvw} (y^c_{(l-1)})^{t\beta(\gamma+v)(\delta+w)} e^{u\beta\gamma\delta}\\ \implies \bm{\delta}_w^{\dagger} &= (\xi_z)_{tuvw} (y^c_{(l-1)})^{t\beta(\gamma+v)(\delta+w)} e_{u\beta\gamma\delta} \\ &= (\xi^{\dagger}_z{}^T)^{utvw} (y^c_{(l-1)}{}^T)^{\beta t(\gamma+v)(\delta+w)} e_{u\beta\gamma\delta} \\ &= \bm{\xi}^T_z \ltimes \bm{y}^c_{(l-1)}{}^T \\ &\equiv (\bm{y}^c_{(l-1)}{}^T \rtimes \bm{\xi}^T_z)^T \\ \end{align*} $$
QED
