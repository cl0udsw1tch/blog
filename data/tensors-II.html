Tensors II:
Universality

The last article dealt with the tensor product construction , now it's time to see what this construction is good for, and how to use only what we care about the construction and forget about the unimportant details. We then extend the theory to larger multilinear maps $f: V_1 \otimes \ldots \otimes V_k \to Y$.

Universality of the Tensor Product

Strictly speaking, the tensor product of $V,W$ is a 2-tuple $(U, u \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, U))$ such that $$\forall Y,f \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, Y), !\exists T_f \in \text{Hom}(U, Y)\mid T_f \circ u = f$$We will call this the universal property of the tensor product. Our $U = V \otimes W := F^{(V \times W)} / S$, with $u = \otimes := v,w \mapsto [\varepsilon_{v,w}]$ is technically just one example of $(U,u)$ which satisfies the UP. We say
"$(U, u)$ where $u \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, U)$ satisfies the universal property for bilinear maps IFF all bilinear maps from $V \times W \to Y$ factor uniquely through $u$ to a unique linear map $U \to Y$."
If $(U, u \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, U))$ satisfies the UP, we say $u$ is "universal" for bilinear maps. One may ask, can we find $\tilde{U}, \tilde{u}$ where $\tilde{u}$ is not in $\text{Bil}(V \times W, \tilde{U})$ but more broadly is a map $V \times W \to \tilde{U}$ that also satisfies the UP? Technically, yes, but there is always a bilinear map hidden somewhere. First, let's call such $\tilde{u}$ "common maps", to distinguish them from "universal maps" which are restricted to be the bilinear ones aforementioned.
Let $(\tilde{U}, \tilde{u}: V \times W \to \tilde{U})$ be such that $\tilde{u}$ is a common map for bilinear maps $V \times W \to Y$. That is, $$\forall p \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, Y), \exists ! \tilde{f} \in \text{Hom}(\tilde{U}, Y) \mid p = \tilde{f} \circ \tilde{u} $$ Then $\tilde{u}(v,w) = z_{u(v, w)} \oplus k$ for the universal bilinear map $u \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, U)$ where $u = \pi_K \circ \tilde{u}$, and $$\begin{align*} U = \tilde{U} / K \\ \pi_K: \tilde{U} \to \tilde{U} / K \\ x \mapsto [x] \\ K = \bigcap_{\tilde{f} \circ \tilde{u} \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, Y)} Ker(\tilde{f}) \end{align*} $$ and $z_{u(v, w)}$ is a fixed representative for each coset $u(v, w)$.
Proof
Suppose the hypothesis. Let $S := \{c(v, w) - (cv, w), \ldots\} $ be the bilinear relations kernel. Firstly, $$ \bigcap_{p \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, Y)} ker(p) = S $$ Notice $$ \begin{align*} (v,w) \in S \implies p(v,w) = \tilde{f} \circ \tilde{u} (v,w) = 0 \implies \tilde{u}(v,w) \in ker(\tilde{f})\\ \implies \tilde{u}(S) \subset ker(\tilde{f}) \implies \tilde{u}(S) \subset K \end{align*}$$ By the first isomorphism theorem, there is a unique and linear $f$ for each $\tilde{f}$ such that $\tilde{f} = f \circ \pi_K$. Thus $$ p = f \circ \pi_K \circ \tilde{u} $$ But now notice $$(v,w) \in S \implies \pi_K \circ \tilde{u}(v,w) = \pi_K (\tilde{u}(v,w)) \in \pi_K(\tilde{u}(S)) \subset \pi_K(K) \\ \implies \pi_K \circ \tilde{u}(v,w) = [0] \\ \implies u:=\pi_K \circ \tilde{u} \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, U := \tilde{U} / K) $$ and since $f$ was unique for the unique $\tilde{f}$, we have that $(U=\tilde{U}/K, u=\pi_K \circ \tilde{u})$ satisfies the UP for bilinear maps. Also $$u(v,w) = \tilde{u}(v,w) + K \implies \tilde{u}(v,w) = z_{u(v, w)} \oplus k$$
QED
This motivates restricting universal morphisms $u$ to $\text{Bil}(V \times W, Y)$ instead of more broadly considering arbitrary maps $V \times W \to Y$, since even if we did that, we would just be writing a direct sum of a universal morphism with the kernel of all the induced linear maps. The opposite implication of the above theorem is true as well.
Let $u \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, U)$ be any universal morphism for bilinear maps. That is $$ \forall p \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, Y), \exists ! f \in \text{Hom}(U, Y) \mid p = f \circ u $$ Let $K$ be any vector subspace and define $$ \begin{align*} \tilde{U} = U \oplus K \\ \tilde{u}: V \times W \to \tilde{U}, \quad v,w \mapsto u(v, w) \oplus k_{v,w} \end{align*} $$ where $k_{v,w} \in K$. Then $\tilde{u}$ is a common morphism for bilinear maps, that is $$ \forall p \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, Y), \exists ! f \in \text{Hom}(\tilde{U}, Y) \mid p = \tilde{f} \circ \tilde{U} $$
Proof
Suppose the hypothesis. $$ \begin{align*} \tilde{u}(v, w) = u(v,w) \oplus k_{v,w} \\ \text{Consider } \pi_{\{0_U\} \oplus K}: \tilde{U} \to \tilde{U} / (\{0_U\} \oplus K), \quad \tilde{u}(v, w) \mapsto \tilde{u}(v, w) + (0_U \oplus K) \\ = u(v, w) \oplus k_{v, w} + (0_U \oplus K) \\ = u(v, w) \oplus 0_K + (0_U \oplus K) \\ = [u(v, w) \oplus 0_K]\\ \text{and } \varphi: \tilde{U} / (\{0_U\} \oplus K) \to U, \quad [u(v, w) \oplus 0_K] \mapsto u(v,w) \end{align*} $$ Notice $u = \varphi \circ \pi_{\{0_U\} \oplus K} \circ \tilde{u}$. By hypothesis, for any $p \in \text{Bil}(V\times W, Y)$ we can find a unique $f \in \text{Hom}(U, Y)$ such that $$ p = f \circ u = f \circ \varphi \circ \pi_{\{0_U\} \oplus K} \circ \tilde{u} = (f \circ \varphi \circ \pi_{\{0_U\} \oplus K} ) \circ \tilde{u} $$ so defining $\tilde{f} = (f \circ \varphi \circ \pi_{\{0_U\} \oplus K} )$ we have a unique linear $\tilde{f}$ for each $p$ satisfying the UP for $\tilde{u}$, so $\tilde{u}$ is a common morphism for bilinear maps.
QED
Let's look at a contrived example. Let $V = \reals^3, W = \reals^{2 \times 2}$. Let $a = \begin{bmatrix}1&0&1&0&0 \end{bmatrix}^T, b = \begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&1&0 \end{bmatrix}^T$ and take $K = \text{span}\{a, b \} \subset \reals^5$. Consider $U = V \otimes W, u(v,w) = v \otimes w$, and define $\tilde{U} = U \oplus K$, with $$\tilde{u}: V \times W \to Y \\ (v, w) \mapsto u(v, w) \oplus k_{v,w} = v \otimes w \oplus \begin{bmatrix} ||v|| & 0 & ||v|| & \det(w) & 0 \end{bmatrix}^T$$ Notice $k_{v,w}$ is indeed in $K$. Let $p \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, Y)$ be arbitrary. Then there exists a unique $f \in \text{Hom}(V \times W, Y) \mid p = f \circ u$. Now let $\tilde{f}= f \circ \varphi \circ \pi_{\{0_U\} \oplus K}$, then $p = \tilde{f} \circ \tilde{u}$ so $u$ is common. Denote $\tilde{x} = x \oplus k \in U \oplus K$ $$ \tilde{f}(\tilde{x}) = f(\varphi(\pi_{\{0_U\} \oplus K}(\tilde{x})) \\ = f(\varphi(\pi_{\{0_U\} \oplus K}(x \oplus k_{v, w}))) \\ = f(\varphi([x + 0_K])) \\ = f(x) $$ so $\tilde{f}(x \oplus k) = f(x)$, that is, linear maps corresponding to bilinear ones factoring through common maps are just the linear maps induced by the universal bilinear map restricted to $U$. Using pair notation $$ \tilde{f}: V \otimes W \oplus K \to Y \\ \tilde{f}(v\otimes w, k) = f(v \otimes w) $$ is the unique linear map for $p$ such that $p = \tilde{f}\circ \tilde{u}$. The nasty non-bilinear part of $\tilde{u}$ does not matter as it can be easily quotiented out as long as it lives in a subspace that direct sums with $U$.

A nice identification

Alright, the actual reason for demanding universal morphisms for bilinear maps be bilinear is a bit deeper, but actually simpler. I just wanted to motivate it in a different direction by showing even non bilinear "common" maps have a bilinear "component" with respect to the direct sum. The actual reason is given by the following claim
$$\exists (U, u) \text{ universal } \iff \text{Bil}(V \times W, Y) \cong \text{Hom}(U, Y) \text{ naturally } $$ That is, universal morphisms for bilinear maps, which themselves are bilinear by definition, imply and are implied by the equivalency between bilinear maps and linear ones on $U$. Thus we care way more about unversal morphisms than common ones so that we can simultaneously show this identification. As well, the term "naturally" means the isomorphisms respect linear morphisms between any two $X,Y $ in the following sense: if $\eta_{X}: \text{Bil}(V \times W, X) \to \text{Hom}(U, X)$ and $\eta_Y: \text{Bil}(V \times W, Y) \to \text{Hom}(U, Y)$ are both isomorphisms, then the family $\{\eta_{-}\}$ is called natural if for any $l \in \text{Hom}( X, Y)$ we have $$ \eta_Y(l \circ p) = l \circ \eta_{X}(p) $$ for all $p \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, X)$
Proof
$(\implies)$ Let $(U, u)$ is a universal for bilinear maps. Then $u \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, U)$. Now notice the map $$\eta_Y: \text{Bil}(V \times W, Y) \to \text{Hom}(U, Y) \\ f \circ u \mapsto f $$ is an isomorphism. To see this, notice it's invertibility. The definition and injectivity is implied by the UP of $u$, since all bilinear maps have the form $f \circ u$. Surjectivity follows since given any linear $f$, $f \circ u$ is bilinear. Thus $\eta_Y$ is our isomorphism, and $$\eta_U(I_U \circ u) = I_U \implies u = \eta_U^{-1}(I_U) $$ where $I_U(x) = x$ is the identity on $U$. Naturally follows since for any $p \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, X)$ there exists a unique $f \in \text{Hom}(U, X)$ such that $p = f \circ u$ and for any $l \in \text{Hom}(X, Y)$ $$ \eta_Y(l \circ p) = \eta_Y(l \circ f \circ u) = \eta_Y((l \circ f) \circ u) = l \circ f = l \circ \eta_X(f \circ u) = l \circ \eta_X(p) $$
$(\impliedby)$ Assume $\text{Bil}(V \times W, Y) \cong \text{Hom}(U, Y)$ naturally. Let the isomorphism be $\eta_Y: \text{Bil}(V \times W, Y) \to \text{Hom}(U, Y)$. Then $\eta_U$ is invertible, and $\eta_U^{-1}(I_U) \in \text{Bil}(V\times W, U)$. Take $u=\eta_U^{-1}(I_U)$. Let $p \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, Y)$. $\eta_Y$ is an isomorphism so there exists a unique $f \in \text{Hom}(U, Y)$ such that $$ \eta_Y(p) = f = f \circ I_U = f \circ \eta_U(\eta^{-1}_U(I_U)) = f \circ \eta_U(u) $$ which by naturality equals $\eta_Y(f \circ u)$ $$\implies \eta^{-1}_Y(\eta_Y(p)) = \eta^{-1}_Y(f \circ u) \implies p = f \circ u $$ $\implies (U,u)$ is universal for bilinear maps.
QED
So universal bilinear maps are a way to construct a natural isomorphism between bilinear maps and homomorphisms, and vice-versa. One might further ask, since we can pick different constructions for universal maps, just how "different" are two universal $(U,u)$? Not very.
Universal bilinear pairs $(U,u)$ are unique up to unique isomorphism.
That is, if $(U, u)$ and $(\tilde{U}, \tilde{u})$ are both universal pairs for bilinear maps, then there is a unique isomorphism $\zeta: U \to \tilde{U}$ such that $$ \zeta \circ u = \tilde{u} $$
Assume the hypothesis, for any $Y$ we have $$ \forall p \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, Y), \exists ! f \in \text{Hom}(U, Y) \mid p = f \circ u \\ \forall p \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, Y), \exists ! \tilde{f} \in \text{Hom}(\tilde{U}, Y) \mid p = \tilde{f} \circ \tilde{u} \\ $$ $\tilde{u} \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, \tilde{U})$ so by the unversality of $(U, u)$, there exists a unique $\zeta \in \text{Hom}(U, \tilde{U}) $ such that $$ \tilde{u} = \zeta \circ u $$ The uniqueness of $\zeta$ means this is our only candidate morphism. To show $\zeta$ is an isomorphism, note that $u \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, U)$ so by the unversality of $(\tilde{U}, \tilde{u})$, there exists a unique $\gamma \in \text{Hom}(\tilde{U}, U) $ such that $$ u = \gamma \circ \tilde{u} $$ so $$ \tilde{u} = \zeta \circ (\gamma \circ \tilde{u}) = (\zeta \circ \gamma) \circ \tilde{u} \\ u = \gamma \circ (\zeta \circ u) = (\gamma \circ \zeta) \circ u $$ Since $\tilde{u} \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, \tilde{U})$, and by the universality of $\tilde{u}$, $\zeta \circ \gamma$ must be the unique morphism in $\text{Hom}(\tilde{U}, \tilde{U})$ satisfying $\tilde{u} = (-) \circ \tilde{u} $, however we also know that $$ \tilde{u} = I_{\tilde{U}} \circ \tilde{u} $$ so by uniqueness $\zeta \circ \gamma = I_{\tilde{U}}$. Similarly, since $u \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, U)$ and by the universality of $u$ it follows $\gamma \circ \zeta = I_U$. Thus, $\gamma$ is the inverse of $\zeta$ and $\zeta$ is an isomorphism.
QED
The reverse is true too.
If $\zeta$ is any isomorphism from $U \to \tilde{U}$, and $(U, u)$ is a universal pair for bilinear maps, then $$ \tilde{u} = \zeta \circ u $$ is a universal morphism, that is, $(\tilde{U}, \tilde{u})$ is another universal pair.
Suppose the hypothesis. Let $p \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, Y)$ be arbitrary. By universality of $u$ $$ \exists ! f \in \text{Hom}(U, Y) \mid p = f \circ u \\ \implies p = f \circ \zeta^{-1} \circ \tilde{u} $$ since $f$ is unique and $\zeta$ is an isomorphism, $\tilde{f} = f \circ \zeta^{-1}$ is the unique morphism in $\text{Hom}(\tilde{U}, Y)$ such that $p = \tilde{f} \circ \tilde{u}$. Thus, $\tilde{u} \in \text{Bil}(V \times W, \tilde{U})$ is a universal morphism for bilinear maps, with $(\tilde{U}, \tilde{u})$ the universal pair.
QED
If $(U, u)$ is a universal pair for bilinear maps, all automorphisms $a$ on $U$ give new universal morphisms $a \circ u$.
This means, for example (with everything over $\reals$), the autmorphism $a: V \otimes W \to V \otimes W$ defined by $$ v \otimes w \mapsto 2 v \otimes w$$ and linearly extended on $V \otimes W$ gives a new universal morphism $\tilde{u}(v, w) = 2 v \otimes w$. This should be clear, since if $f$ was the unique linear map composed with $\otimes$ to give some bilinear map, using this new univeral morphism means we just need to half $f$ to get the corresponding morphism. If $a$ was more complicated, then we would invert it instead, which we can do since it's an automorphism.

Features and Generalizations

Let's actually forget our free-space-quotient construction of the tensor product and use an arbitrary universal morphism for bilinear maps from now on. Universality is all we'll need to use to further investigate the tensor product; plus, since $(U, u)$ is unique up to unique isomorphism, all constructions are unique up to isomorphism anyway. We need to redefine our notation; let $(U, u)$ be universal for bilinear maps over $V \times W$, and define $$ V \otimes W := U \\ v \otimes w := u(v, w) $$ That is, we are divorcing $\otimes$ from the free-space-quotient construction. We can then say
"$(V \otimes W, (v,w) \mapsto v \otimes w)$ is the unique (up to unique isomorphism) universal pair for bilinear maps."
We now show a very important statement, that universal bilinear maps generate universal multilinear maps of higher degree. Firstly, since the domain for multilinear maps can now be an arbitrary number of spaces $V_1 \ldots V_n$ we need a way to denote them. We will call the [up to unique isomorphism] universal multilinear pair $(M, m)[V_1 \ldots V_k] = (M[V_1 \ldots V_k], m[V_1 \ldots V_k])$ which is taken to be universal for multilinear maps over $V_1 \ldots V_k$ - the first object being the vector space and the second being the universal morphism. This pair is unique up to unique isomorphism for $V_1 \ldots V_k$. We will use the special notation $(B,b)[V_1, V_2] = (B[V_1,V_2], b[V_1, V_2]) = (M[V_1,V_2], m[V_1, V_2])$ for universal bilinear pairs. We will write $(M_1^i, m_1^i) = (M[V_1 \ldots V_i], m[V_1 \ldots V_i])$ and use $B,b$ when there are only two spaces in the domain.
Universal bilinear pairs generate universal multilinear pairs of higher degree. Formally, $$ (B[M_1^i, M_{i+1}^{i+j}], b[M_1^i, M_{i+1}^{i+j}](m_1^i(-), m_{i+1}^{i+j}(-)) = (M[V_1 \ldots V_{i+j}], m[V_1 \ldots V_{i+j}]) $$ is universal for multilinear maps over $V_1 \ldots V_{i+j}$.
Proof
Suppose the hypothesis. Let $t \in \text{Mul}(V_1 \times \ldots \times V_{i+j}, Y)$ be arbitrary. Importantly we are not going to apriori assume any construction of universal bilinear pairs for multilinear maps of this degree; we will construct it from $(M_1^i, m_1^i)$ and $(M_{i+1}^{i+j}, m_{i+1}^{i+j})$ instead. To do this, fix $v_{i+1} \ldots v_{i+j} \in V_{i+1} \times \ldots \times V_{i+j}$. Then $$ r_{v_{i+1} \ldots v_{i+j}}(v_1 \ldots v_i) := t(v_1 \ldots v_{i+j}) $$ is a $i$-linear map over $V_1 \ldots V_i$, for which we have a universal mulitilinear map to uniquely factor it through by hypothesis. Then $$ r_{v_{i+1} \ldots v_{i+j}}(v_1 \ldots v_i) = f_{v_{i+1} \ldots v_{i+j}} \circ m_1^i(v_1 \ldots v_i) $$ for some unique $ f_{v_{i+1} \ldots v_{i+j}} \in \text{Hom}(M_1^i, Y)$. So we have $$ t(v_1 \ldots v_{i+j}) = f_{v_{i+1} \ldots v_{i+j}} \circ m_1^i(v_1 \ldots v_i) $$ Since the map $v_{i+1} \ldots v_{i+j} \mapsto r_{v_{i+1} \ldots v_{i+j}}$ is $j$-linear, so is the map $v_{i+1} \ldots v_{i+j} \mapsto f_{v_{i+1} \ldots v_{i+j}}$ (convince yourself of this before continuing). Now let $$ l_{x}(v_{i+1} \ldots v_{i+j}) := f_{v_{i+1} \ldots v_{i+j}}(x) $$ Note, the map $x \mapsto l_x$ is linear. Also, since $v_{i+1} \ldots v_{i+j} \mapsto f_{v_{i+1} \ldots v_{i+j}}$ is $j$-linear, this map $l_x$ must be $j$-linear as well, over $V_{i+1} \ldots V_{i+j}$, so by universality of $m_{i+1}^{i+j}$, $$ f_{v_{i+1} \ldots v_{i+j}}(x) = l_x(v_{i+1} \ldots v_{i+j}) = g_{x} \circ m_{i+1}^{i+j}(v_{i+1} \ldots v_{i+j}) $$ for some unique $g_x \in \text{Hom}(M_{i+1}^{i+j}, Y)$. Note that $x \mapsto l_x$ being linear implies $x \mapsto g_x$ is linear as well. We now have, $$ t(v_1 \ldots v_{i+j}) = f_{v_{i+1} \ldots v_{i+j}}(m_1^i(v_1 \ldots v_i)) = g_{m_1^i(v_1 \ldots v_i)} \circ m_{i+1}^{i+j}(v_{i+1} \ldots v_{i+j}) $$ But $x \mapsto g_x$ being linear and $g_x$ itself being linear means the map $$ G(x,y) = g_x(y) $$ is bilinear, and $$ t(v_1 \ldots v_{i+j}) = G(m_1^i(v_1 \ldots v_i), m_{i+1}^{i+j}(v_{i+1} \ldots v_{i+j}) ) $$ Bilinearity of $G$ means it factors uniquely over the universal bilinear map; that is $$ G(m_1^i(v_1 \ldots v_i), m_{i+1}^{i+j}(v_{i+1} \ldots v_{i+j}) ) = h \circ b[M_1^i, M_{i+1}^{j+1}](m_1^i(v_1 \ldots v_i), m_{i+1}^{i+j}(v_{i+1} \ldots v_{i+j})) $$ for some unique $h \in \text{Hom}(B[M_1^i, M_{i+1}^{i+j}], Y)$. Thus, $$ t(v_1 \ldots v_{i+j}) = h \circ b[M_1^i, M_{i+1}^{j+1}](m_1^i(v_1 \ldots v_i), m_{i+1}^{i+j}(v_{i+1} \ldots v_{i+j})) $$ for a unique $h$, so $$ (B[M_1^i, M_{i+1}^{j+1}], b[M_1^i, M_{i+1}^{j+1}](m_1^i(-), m_{i+1}^{j+1}(-)) $$ is universal for $(i+j)$-linear maps.
QED
The universal bilinear pair is associative. That is $$ (B[B[V_1, V_2], V_3], b[B[V_1, V_2], V_3](b[V_1, V_2](-), b[V_3](-)))\\ = (B[V_1, B[V_2, V_3]],b[V_1, B[V_2, V_3]](b[V_1](-), b[V_2,V_3](-))) $$ up to unique isomorphism. This follows from the previous theorem by taking $i=2, j=1$ and then $i=1, j=2$ and noting both constructions are universal for trilinear functions over $V_1\times V_2 \times V_3$. Note that the universal map for linear functions is just the identity. So $B[V_i] = V_i, b[V_i] = I_{V_i}$.
It's at this point where it would be wise to see an example to consolidate what we've been discussing. Associativity for example means $$ \phi: V_1 \times V_2 \times V_3 \to (V_1 \otimes V_2) \otimes V_3 \\ v_1,v_2,v_3 \mapsto (v_1 \otimes v_2) \otimes v_3 $$ and $$ \psi: V_1 \times V_2 \times V_3 \to V_1 \otimes (V_2 \otimes V_3) \\ v_1,v_2,v_3 \mapsto v_1 \otimes (v_2 \otimes v_3) $$ are unique up to unique isomorphism, and both morphisms are universal for trilinear functions on $V_1 \times V_2 \times V_3$. The theorem as a whole shows that the bilinear universal $\otimes$ can generate morphisms that are universal for multilinear maps of any degree we choose, so for example, if we want a universal morphism for $5$-linear functions on $V_1 \times \ldots \times V_5$, and we had $\otimes, \psi$ as $2$ and $3$-linear universals over $V_1 \times V_2$ and $V_3 \times V_4 \times V_5$ respectively then $$ \omega: (V_1 \otimes V_2) \times (V_3 \otimes V_4 \otimes V_5) \to (V_1 \otimes V_2) \otimes (V_3 \otimes (V_4 \otimes V_5)) \\ v_1\ldots v_5 \mapsto (v_1 \otimes v_2) \otimes (v_3 \otimes (v_4 \otimes v_5)) $$ is such a $5$-linear universal. All such $1\leq i\leq j = 5-i$ are valid with the theorem, and all produce $5$-linear universals over $V_1 \ldots V_5)$; the parenthesis placement changes the universal pair only up to unique isomorphism, so all can be removed. Thus $n$-repeated use of $\otimes$ is taken to denote $\text{\it the}$ (up to unique isomorphism) universal map or space for $n$-linear maps and for example we can write $\omega$ as $$ \omega(v_1 \ldots v_5) = v_1 \otimes \ldots \otimes v_5 $$

The Multilinear Generalization (and a bit of practical exposition)

I could end the article here, but there's a consequence of our construction that needs addressing. Let's say you have 5 spaces $V_1 \ldots V_5$ and a universal morphism $\phi$ for $5$-linear maps over their product, something like $$ \phi: v_1 \ldots v_5 \mapsto (v_1 \otimes (v_2 \otimes (v_3 \otimes v_4))) \otimes v_5 \\ $$ This requires 4 separate applications of the universal bilinear map, and is an $O(4)$ operation. That's all fine and well, but what if you discover that the $V_i$ are actually $M[W_{a_i} \ldots W_{b_i}]$ universal spaces over $W_{a_i} \ldots W_{b_i}$ with universal morphism $p_i = m[W_{a_i} \ldots W_{b_i}]$ and you know that $v_i = p_i(w_{a_i} \ldots w_{b_i})$. After substitution, you ask yourself, "do I get a $c$-linear morphism over the $W$ spaces, where $c = \sum c_i = \sum (b_i - a_i + 1)$?" Let's make this concrete, suppose $$ \begin{align*} V_1 &= M[W_1, W_2] \\ V_2 &= M[W_3,W_4,W_5] \\ V_3 &= M[W_6, W_7] \\ V_4 &= M[W_8, W_9, W_{10}]\\ V_5 &= M[W_{11}, W_{12}] \end{align*} $$ We can absolutely derive the $c$-linear morphism; simply start with the inner parentheses and move outward. We know $$ \begin{align*} V_3 \otimes V_4 = M[W_6 \ldots W_{10}] \\ v_3 \otimes v_4 = p_3(w_6, w_7) \otimes p_4(w_8,w_9,w_{10}) \\= m[W_6, W_7](w_6,w_7) \otimes m[W_8,W_9,W_{10}](w_8,w_9,w_{10}) \\ =m[W_6 \ldots W_{10}](w_6 \ldots w_{10}) \end{align*} $$ by our theorem, since $\otimes$ is the bilinear universal. Likewise $$ \begin{align*} V_2 \otimes (V_3 \otimes V_4) = M[W_3,W_4,W_5] \otimes M[W_6 \ldots W_{10}] = M[W_3 \ldots W_{10}] \\ v_2 \otimes (v_3 \otimes v_4) = p_2(w_3,w_4,w_5) \otimes m[W_6 \ldots W_{10}](w_6 \ldots w_{10}) \\ = m[W_3, W_4, W_{5}](w_3,w_4,w_5) \otimes m[W_6 \ldots W_{10}](w_6 \ldots w_{10}) \\ = m[W_3 \ldots W_{10}](w_3 \ldots w_{10}) \\ \vdots \end{align*} $$ and continuing until we show $p_1(w_1, w_2) \ldots p_5(w_{11}, w_{12}) \mapsto m[W_1 \ldots W_{12}](w_1 \ldots w_{12})$ under $\phi$. We would like to not have to execute this procedure to prove similar claims each time. What if instead of $\phi$ being over $V_1 \times \ldots \times V_5$ it was over $V_1 \times \ldots \times V_{200,000,000}$? We solve this issue with the following theorem.
Mutlilinear universal maps generate multilinear universal maps with degree equal to the sum of the degrees of the component maps. That is, if $$(P_i, p_i) = (M[V_{a_i} \ldots V_{b_i}], m[V_{a_i} \ldots V_{b_i}])$$ are universal pairs for $c_i$-linear maps over $V_{a_i} \ldots V_{b_i}$ for $i = 1 \ldots n$, where $c_i = b_i-a_i+1$, and if $$(P, p) = (M[P_1 \ldots P_n], m[P_1 \ldots P_n])$$ is a universal pair for $n$-linear functions over $\prod_{i=1}^n P_i$, then $$ (P, p(p_1(-) \ldots p_n(-))) $$ is the unique (up to unique isomorphism) universal pair for $c$-linear functions over $V_{a_1} \ldots V_{b_n}$, where $c = \sum c_i$.
Proof
We proceed by induction on $n$. The base case $n=2$ is given by the previous theorem with $p$ being the bilinear universal. So suppose the claim holds for $n-1$. Let $t: V_{a_1} \ldots V_{b_n} \to Y$ be an arbitrary $c$-linear function. Define $$ t_{v_{a_n} \ldots v_{b_n}}(v_{a_1} \ldots v_{b_{n-1}}) := t(v_{a_1} \ldots v_{b_n}) $$ This is $(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1} c_i)$-linear, over $V_{a_1} \ldots V_{b_{n-1}}$ so by the inductive hypothesis $$ t_{v_{a_n} \ldots v_{b_n}}(v_{a_1} \ldots v_{b_{n-1}}) = f_{v_{a_n} \ldots v_{b_n}}(\tilde{p}(p_1(v_{a_1} \ldots v_{b_1}) \ldots p_{n-1}(v_{a_{n-1}} \ldots v_{b_{n-1}}))) $$ for some unique $f_{v_{a_n} \ldots v_{b_n}} \in \text{Hom}(\tilde{P}, Y) $ where $$ (\tilde{P}, \tilde{p}) = (M[P_1 \ldots P_{n-1}], m[P_1 \ldots P_{n-1}]) $$ Define $$ g_x(v_{a_n} \ldots v_{b_n}) := f_{v_{a_n} \ldots v_{b_n}}(x) $$ then $$ t(v_{a_1} \ldots v_{b_{n}}) = g_{\tilde{p}(p_1(v_{a_1} \ldots v_{b_1}) \ldots p_{n-1}(v_{a_{n-1}} \ldots v_{b_{n-1}}))}(v_{a_n} \ldots v_{b_n}) \\ = h_{\tilde{p}(p_1(v_{a_1} \ldots v_{b_1}) \ldots p_{n-1}(v_{a_{n-1}} \ldots v_{b_{n-1}}))}(p_n(v_{a_n} \ldots v_{b_n})) $$ for some unique $ h_{\tilde{p}(p_1(v_{a_1} \ldots v_{b_1}) \ldots p_{n-1}(v_{a_{n-1}} \ldots v_{b_{n-1}}))} \in \text{Hom}(P_n, Y)$. Define $H(x, y) = h_x(y)$, then $$ t(v_{a_1} \ldots v_{b_{n}}) = H(\tilde{p}(p_1(v_{a_1} \ldots v_{b_1}) \ldots p_{n-1}(v_{a_{n-1}} \ldots v_{b_{n-1}})), p_n(v_{a_n} \ldots v_{b_n})) \\ = q(\tilde{p}(p_1(v_{a_1} \ldots v_{b_1}) \ldots p_{n-1}(v_{a_{n-1}} \ldots v_{b_{n-1}})) \otimes p_n(v_{a_n} \ldots v_{b_n})) $$ for some unique $q \in \text{Hom}(\tilde{P} \times P_n,Y)$. Thus $$ X = (B[\tilde{P}, P_n], b[\tilde{P}, P_n](\tilde{p}(p_1(-) \ldots p_{n-1}(-)), p_n(-))) $$ is unversal over $V_{a_1} \ldots V_{b_n}$. Substituting for $\tilde{P}, \tilde{p}$ $$ X = (B[M[P_1 \ldots P_{n-1}], P_n], b[M[P_1 \ldots P_{n-1}], P_n](m[P_1 \ldots P_{n-1}](p_1(-) \ldots p_{n-1}(-)), p_n(-))) $$ By the previous theorem for multilinear universal extention through the bilinear universal $$ X = (M[P_1 \ldots P_n], m[P_1 \ldots P_n](p_1(-) \ldots p_n(-)) $$
QED
I hope that wasn't too hairy; the proof isn't particularly complicated it's just that there are alot of different variables everywhere. But the utility should not be lost on the reader. Going back to the earlier task, if $$ \phi(v_1 \ldots v_5) = (v_1 \otimes (v_2 \otimes (v_3 \otimes v_4))) \otimes v_5 $$ is a universal 5-linear morphism from $\prod V_i$, and if later you figure out that each $v_i = p_i(w_{a_i} \ldots w_{b_i}) = m[W_{a_i} \ldots W_{b_i}](w_{a_i} \ldots w_{b_i})$ is the result of some universal multilinear morphism from some other product of spaces, then you can simply substitute these expressions into the above and obtain a multilinear morphism on all the $W_{a_1} \ldots W_{b_5}$ $$ w_{a_1} \ldots w_{b_5} \mapsto \phi(p_1(w_{a_1}\ldots w_{b_1}) \ldots p_5(w_{a_5}\ldots w_{b_5})) \\= \left(\left(\bigotimes_{j=a_1}^{b_1} w_{j}\right) \otimes \left(\left(\bigotimes_{j=a_2}^{b_2} w_{j}\right)\otimes \left(\left(\bigotimes_{j=a_3}^{b_3} w_{j}\right)\otimes \left(\bigotimes_{j=a_4}^{b_4} w_{j}\right)\right)\right)\right) \otimes \left(\bigotimes_{j=a_5}^{b_5} w_{j}\right) $$ and conclude using the above theorem that the above map is indeed a multilinear universal morphism on the arguments.
