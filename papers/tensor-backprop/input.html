Tensorial Formulation of Backpropagation

The matrix formulation of both feed-foward neural networks and convolutional neural networks (and other networks as well, for example, transformers) is inspired by the linear relationship between the parameters of the network. However, when the dimensions of the space the parameters live in begins to grow, and so do the relationships between parameters, matrices become an inappropriate tool to mathematically describe the model and a rather clumsy tool to describe the backpropogation algorithm, so here, tensors are chosen instead.

1. Feed Forward Neural Networks

We will call a neural network a layered model that produces features of samples in a "linear" way, by using parameters and the sample features at previous layers and then applying a non-linear transform. Define the following:
  • Dataset: $$\mathcal{D} = \{(x^i, v^i)\}_{i \in I} $$ for some index set $I$, where $x^i \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is a sample with $n$ features, and $v^i \in \mathbb{R}$ is its label
  • The $j$th feature of the $i$th sample produced at hidden layer $l-1$, feeding into hidden layer $l$. $$(y_{l})^{i,j}$$
Suppose now we have $(y_{l-1})^{i,j}$ for every $j$ in some index set $\mathcal{V}_{l-1}$. We aim to produce [one of] the next features $(y_{l})^{i,k}$ for the same sample $i$; namely feature number $k \in \mathcal{V}_l$ not necessarily equal to $j$. The feed-forward neural network (FFN) achieves this in two steps:
  1. We take a set of parameters $\{(W_l)^{j,k} \in \mathbb{R}\}_{j \in \mathcal{V}_{l-1}} \cup \{(b_l)^k\}$ - we may also call these weights and biases - and multiply each to the respective $j$th feature of the $i$th sample at layer $l-1$, before summing. That is, we produce $$ \begin{equation} (z_l)^{i,k} = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{V}_{l-1}}(y_{l-1})^{i,j} (W_l)^{j,k} + (b_l)^k \end{equation} $$ a "preimage" of the $k$th feature of the $i$th sample at layer $l$. Just the preimage thus far, since at step 2...
  2. a smooth, nonlinear transform from $C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R})$ is henceforth applied: $$ \begin{equation} (y_l)^{i,k} = f_l((z_l)^{i,k}) \end{equation} $$ producing the desired feature.

Tensors

It is at this point we will specify important details needed to understand the rest of the article. Firstly, we will use Einstein's Summation Convention (ESC) to simplify sums with a repeated index, signifying the summation of the terms over said index. For example, we can rewrite (1) as $$ \begin{equation} (z_l)^{i,k} = (y_{l-1})^{i,j} (W_l)^{j,k} + b^k \end{equation} $$ where the sum is meant to be over $j$, which repeats, and where all terms are zero outside of their domains. This too deserves formalization. Firstly, let's consider $(y_l)$ as a well defined object, namely, a tensor over infinite dimensional vector spaces, but with finite support. Let's clarify, since the $(y_l)^{a,b}$ objects encompass $\mathcal{V}_l$ features for $n$ samples, lets define $$ \begin{align} y_l &= \sum_{i,k} (y_l)^{i,k} e_i \otimes e_k \in \mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}} \otimes \mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}} \\ W_l &= \sum_{j,k} (W_l)^{j,k} e_j \otimes e_k \in \mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}} \otimes \mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}} \\ b_l &= \sum_{i,k} (b_l)^{i,k} e_i \otimes e_k \in \mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}} \otimes \mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}} \end{align} $$ where $(b_l)^{i,k} = (b_l)^{i',k} = (b_l)^k $ for all $i,i'$.
Throughout the article, $V = \mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}}$ and so $y_l,W_l,z_l,b_l \in V \otimes V$.
We will also make use of Abstract Index Notation (AIN), which is particularly useful when representing the shape of a tensor. For example, we could write $(y_l)^{ab}$ to denote that $y_l \in V \otimes V$, or for a more general example $$ T^{ab}{}_{c}{}^{d}{}_{e} $$ to denote a tensor $T \in V \otimes V \otimes V^* \otimes V \otimes V^*$.
We proceed from the tensorized version of (1) $$ \begin{align} (z_l)^{i,k} e_i \otimes e_k &= ((y_{l-1})^{i,j} (W_l)^{j,k} + (b_l)^{i,k} ) e_i \otimes e_k \\ \implies z_l &= \sum_{i,j,k} (y_{l-1})^{i,j} (W_l)^{j,k} e_i \otimes e_k + \sum_{i,k} (b_l)^{i,k} e_i \otimes e_k \in V \otimes V \end{align} $$ We also need to transform the $C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R})$ transform into one that acts on tensors, that is one from $C^{\infty}(V \otimes V, V \otimes V )$ by defining $$ \begin{equation} y_l = F_l(z_l) = \sum_{i,k} f_l((z_l)^{i,k}) e_i \otimes e_k \end{equation} $$

Contraction, Inner Products, Metric Tensors


The issue with (8) is that is kind of useless; we want to speak in a language of tensors and yet are using the coefficients of the layer features and parameters, instead of working with their tensor representations directly. Using basic linear algebra, one realizes that (8) can be formulated as a matrix product, where the inner product is over the space indexed by $j$. Using tensors however, we see (6) as the image of the metric tensor, or, if one of the $j$ were lowered, as a contraction. Before jumping in, we need to agree on contraction notation; in (AIN) this is straighforward: if it had been the case that our tensors were of mixed $(1,1)$ rank, we could write $$ \begin{equation} (z_l)^a{}_c = (y_{l-1})^a{}_b (W_l)^b{}_c + (b_l)^a{}_c \end{equation} $$ However, we actually want to write contraction as a well-formed function, since we will need to derivate later, so lets agree on the convention
$$C_{i_1 \ldots i_{\phi}}^{i'_1 \ldots i'_{\phi}}(T) := \text{contraction over covector and contravector slot } i_j, i'_j \text{ for each } j $$
assuming of course $T$ is homogenous over the $i$th and $j$th slots (i.e they are over the same vector space up-to duality). Rewriting the equation for our $(1,1)$ tensors: $$ \begin{equation} z_l = C^3_2(y_l \otimes W_l) + b_l \end{equation} $$
Unfortunately our tensors arent of rank $(1,1)$ and the shared index across $y_{l-1}, W_l$ is across $(V, V)$ not $(V^*, V)$, so the operation we're looking for is the inner-product, or, contraction against a metric tensor. We should talk briefly about the metric tensor $g_{ab}$ and the inverse metric tensor $ {g}^{ab}$. $V$ has the canonical orthonormal basis, and is endowed with the canonical inner product. For $V$, the basis is $\{e_i\}$ and the inner product is given by $\langle e_i, e_j \rangle = \delta_{ij}$. For the dual space $V^*$, the basis is $\{e^i\}$ with inner product $\langle e^i, e^j \rangle = \delta_{ij} $. The metric tensors are then given by agreement with the inner products $$\begin{align} g_{ab} = g_{i,j} e^i \otimes e^j = \delta_{ij} e^i \otimes e^j \\ {g}^{ab} = g^{i,j} e_i \otimes e_j = \delta_{ij} e_i \otimes e_j \end{align}$$ Typically we would fully contract either tensor against the product of two input tensors, however we are going to need to denote partially contracting against the metric tensor. Thus let us generalize the previously mentioned metrics using maps
$$ G_{i_1 \ldots i_{\phi}, i'_1 \ldots i'_{\phi}}^{j_1 \ldots j_{\psi}, j'_1 \ldots j'_{\psi}}(A,B) $$ $$ = C_{a_1 \ldots a_{\phi}, b_1 \ldots b_{\phi}, j_1 \ldots j_{\psi}, j'_1 \ldots j'_{\psi}}^{i_1 \ldots i_{\phi}, i'_1 \ldots i'_{\phi},c_1 \ldots c_{\psi}, d_1 \ldots d_{\psi}}(\bigotimes_{m\leq \phi} g_{a_mb_m} \bigotimes_{n \leq \psi} g^{c_nd_n} \otimes A \otimes B) $$ $$ = g_{i_1i'_1}\ldots g_{i_{\phi}i'_{\phi}} g^{j_1j'_1} \ldots g^{j_{\psi}j'_{\psi}}AB$$ $$= \text{contraction of } A \otimes B \text{ against each } g_{ab} \text{ over contravariant slots } i_p, i'_p $$ $$ \text{and } g^{ab} \text{ over covariant slots } j_q, j'_q \text{ of } A,B \text{ resp. } $$
Now we can find a suitable tensorial representation for (8) in terms of tensors, not coefficients.
$$ z_l = (y_{l-1})^{i,j}(W_l)^{j,k}e_i \otimes e_k + (b_l)^{i,k} e_i \otimes e_k= G_{b,c}((y_{l-1})^{ab}, (W_l)^{cd}) + (b_l)^{ad} $$

The Model

We now formalize the FNN model $\mathcal{F}$ completely.

  • We will call a layer $l$ the function $$y_{l-1} \mapsto \mathcal{L}_l(y_{l-1} ; W_l, b_l) = \Gamma_l(y_{l-1}, W_l, b_l)$$ where $$ \begin{equation} \Gamma_{l}(S, T, U) = F_l(g_{bc}S^{ab}T^{cd}+ U) \end{equation} $$ We will call the neuron $k$ at layer $l$ the function $$ y_{l-1} \mapsto \mathcal{N}_{l,k}(y_{l-1} ; W_l, b_l) = \gamma_{l,k}(y_{l-1}, W_l, b_l) $$ where $$ \gamma_{l,k}(S, T, U) = F_l(g_{bc}S^{ab}(T^{jk} e_j \otimes e_k)^{cd}+U^{i,k}e_i \otimes e_k) $$$$ = \Gamma_l(S, T^{jk} e_j \otimes e_k, U^{i,k}e_i \otimes e_k) $$ Note that $$ \sum_k \gamma_{l,k}(S, T, U) = \Gamma_l(S, T, U) $$ that is, a layer is a sum of neurons.
  • First we start with the input layer , $\mathcal{L}_0$. We define $$(y_0)^{i,j} := (x^i)^j $$ $$ x:= \sum_{i,j} (x^i)^{j}e_i \otimes e_j = \sum_{i,j} (y_0)^{i,j}e_i \otimes e_j = y_0 $$ and define $\mathcal{L}_0$ by $x \mapsto y_0$, so $\mathcal{L}_0$ is the identity. It's not strictly a computational layer like defined earlier, however some folks like to speak of an input layer, which refers to this.
  • The hidden layers are $\{\mathcal{L}_l\}_{ l=1\dots L}$
  • The output layer is $$y_L \mapsto \mathcal{L}_{L+1}(y_L; W_{L+1}, b_{L+1}) = F_{L+1}( g_{bc}(y_L)^{ab}(W_{L+1})^{cd} + b_{L+1})$$ where $F_{L+1}$ is called the activation function.
  • The model $\mathcal{F}$ is defined by $$ \mathcal{F}(x ; \theta) = \mathcal{L}_{L+1}(\cdot ; W_{L+1},b_{L+1}) \circ \ldots \circ \mathcal{L}_{1}(\cdot ; W_{1}, b_1) \circ \mathcal{L_0} (x) $$ where $$\theta = W_1,b_1 \ldots W_{L+1},b_{L+1} $$ are the parameters of the model.

Loss, Empirical Risk

We have so far the ability to compute the output of our parameterized model $y_{L+1} = \mathcal{F}(x;\theta)$. Let $X,Y$ be the random variables for input features and labels. Let $$X,Y \sim \mathcal{P}(X,Y) $$ with pdf $p_{X,Y}(x,y)$. Let $X_i,Y_i$ be the ith feature and label random variables, and assume $$X_i, Y_i \sim_{i.i.d} \mathcal{P}(X,Y)$$ Define a loss function $\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}:$ $$ w, \hat{w} \mapsto \Delta(w, \hat{w}) $$ For a model $h$, the loss at a sample $(x^i, v^i) \in \mathcal{D}$ is $$ l(x^i,v^i,h) = \Delta(v^i, h(x^i)) $$ The risk is the expected value of the loss over $X \times Y$ $$ R(h) = \mathbb{E}_{X,Y}(l(x,y,h)) = \int l(x,y,h) p_{X,Y}(x,y) dxdy $$ which is intractable, so the empirical risk is used instead, which, as data size grows, converges to the risk by the LLN: $$ \begin{equation} \hat{R}(d, h) = \frac{1}{I}\sum_i l(x^i,v^i,h) \to \mathbb{E}_{X,Y}(l(x,y,h)) \end{equation} $$as $I\to\infty$ where $d=(x^1,v^1\ldots x^I,v^I)$ is the realized sample. Empirical Risk Minimization chooses a model space, in this case, $\mathcal{H} = \{\mathcal{F}(\cdot;\theta) \}_{\theta}$ and finds $$ \argmin_{h \in \mathcal{H}}\hat{R}(d, h) = \mathcal{F}(\cdot;\argmin_{\theta}\hat{R}(d, \mathcal{F}(\cdot ; \theta))) $$ so we find the $\argmin$ on the RHS, where $$ \argmin_{\theta} \hat{R}(d, \mathcal{F}(\cdot ; \theta)) = \argmin_{\theta}\frac{1}{I}\sum_i\Delta(v^i, \mathcal{F}(x^i ; \theta)) $$

Loss Function, Activation Functions

We will need a way to choose the loss function $\Delta$. While we could make an arbitrary choice, we could also use Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) to derive it. Let $$\mathscr{P}=\{\mathcal{P}(X,Y; \theta) : p_{X,Y}(x,y;\theta) = p_{Y|X}(y|x;\theta)p_X(x) = p_0(y;\mathcal{F}(x;\theta))p_X(x) \}_{\theta \in \Theta }$$ and assume $$X,Y \sim \mathcal{P}(X, Y; {\theta}) \in \mathscr{P} $$ with density $p_{X,Y}(x,y;\theta) = p_0(y;\mathcal{F}(x;\theta))p_X(x)$. The sample distribution for the random vector ${\bf D}= (X_1,Y_1)\ldots(X_I,Y_I)$ is then given by the density $$ p_{{\bf D}}({\bf d};\theta)=_{i.i.d} \prod_i p(x_i, y_i;\theta) = \prod_i p_0(y_i;\mathcal{F}(x_i;\theta))p_X(x_i) $$ The likelihood is $$ \mathbb{L}(\theta; {\bf \mathfrak{d}}) = p_{\bf D}({\bf \mathfrak{d}}; \theta) = \prod_i p_0(v^i;\mathcal{F}(x^i;\theta))p_X(x^i) $$ where ${\bf \mathfrak{d}}$ is a realized sample. Taking the negative log gives $$ -\log(\mathbb{L}(\theta; {\bf \mathfrak{d}})) = -\sum_i \log(p_X(x^i)) - \sum_i \log(p_0(v^i;\mathcal{F}(x^i; \theta))) $$ which we want minimized over $\theta$. To do so, we need only find $$ \begin{equation} \argmin_{\theta}\left(- \sum_i \log(p_0(v^i;\mathcal{F}(x^i; \theta)))\right) \end{equation} $$ and set $$\Delta(a, b) := -\log(p_0(a;b)) $$ $$ \begin{equation}\implies l(x^i, v^i, \mathcal{F}(\cdot;\theta)) = \Delta(v^i, \mathcal{F}(x^i;\theta)) = -\log(p_0(v^i;\mathcal{F}(x^i; \theta))) \end{equation}$$ $$ \implies (16) = I * \argmin_{\theta} \hat{R}(\mathfrak{d},\mathcal{F}(\cdot; \theta))$$ so maximizing the likelihood assuming class $\mathscr{P}$ will also minimize the empirical risk if the loss is given in the form (17). By our assumption on the data and $\mathscr{P}$, $$(Y|X=x) \sim \mathcal{P}_{Y|X}(y|x; \theta) $$ with $$p_{Y|X}(y|x;\theta)=p_0(y;\mathcal{F}(x,\theta))$$ $$\implies (Y|X=x) \sim \mathcal{P}_0(Y;\nu=\mathcal{f}(x;\theta))$$ for some apriori chosen $\mathcal{P}_0$ with density $p_0$. Letting $\nu=\mathcal{f}(x;\theta)$, we now investigate suitable choices for $\mathcal{P}_0$ by the nature of $Y|X=x$:
  • If $(Y|X=x) \in \{0,1\}$ in the case of a binary learning task, then $$\mathcal{P}_0(Y;\nu) = \textbf{Bernoulli}(Y;\nu)$$ is a suitable choice (although actually derived from the axioms of probability) with $$p_0(y;\nu) = \nu^y(1-\nu)^{1-y} $$ $$\implies l(x^i, v^i, \mathcal{F}(\cdot; \theta))= -\log(p_0(v^i, \nu)) = -\left(v^i\log(\nu) + (1-v^i)\log(1-\nu)\right) $$ that is, the Binary Cross Entropy (BCE) loss.
  • If $(Y|X=x) \in S \subset \mathbb{Z} : \lvert S \rvert < \infty$ more broadly, in the case of categorical learning, then (noting $\nu$ is a vector $\nu^c e_c$) $$ \mathcal{P}_0(Y;\nu)=\textbf{Categorical}(Y;\nu) $$ is a suitable choice (although actually derived from the axioms of probability) with $$p_0(y;\nu)=\prod_c (\nu^c)^{[y=c]}$$
    • $$\implies l(x^i, v^i, \mathcal{F}(\cdot; \theta))= -\log(p_0(v^i, \nu)) =-\sum_c [v^i=c]\log(\nu^c) $$ that is, the Categorical Cross Entropy (CCE) loss.
  • If $(Y|X=x) \in \mathbb{R}$ in the case of a regression, then $$ \mathcal{P}_0(Y;\nu)=\mathcal{N}(Y;\mu=\nu,\sigma^2=1) $$ is a suitable choice with $$p_0(y;\nu)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left({-\frac{(y-\nu)^2}{2}}\right)$$ $$\implies l(x^i, v^i, \mathcal{F}(\cdot; \theta))= -\log(p_0(v^i, \nu)) =-\log(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}) + \frac{1}{2}(v^i - \nu)^2 $$ although note to minimize the above, one need only minimize the Mean Squared Error (MSE) term $(v^i-\nu)^2$.
