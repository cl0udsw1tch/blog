Tensors

Tensors are often thought of as multidimensional arrays, especially in programming with NumPy and general Machine Learning tasks. The reader may also be aware of their usage in physics (general relativity) and engineering. In this article we explore the motivation for creating and using tensors in a way that reintroduces structure often glossed over in introductory examples.

The MQ (Multilinear Question)

We start with a question: for vector spaces $V, W, Y$, can a multilinear map $f \in \text{Mul}(V \times W, Y)$ be identified as a linear map $T_f \in \text{Hom}(X, W)$ for some $X$? Actually better yet, since $X$ is $\it produced$ from $V, W$ we can rephrase our question as this: $\newline$ Is there such a $\it product$, lets call $\otimes$, such that any $f \in \text{Mul}(V \times W, Y)$ can be identified as some unique linear map $T_f \in \text{Hom}(V \otimes W, Y)?$ $\newline$
It turns out, the answer is yes. We offer one such construction of $V \otimes W$. Before doing that however, lets try and simulate how a student might approach this problem.

An approach

An ambitious student might try and see if there is some relation imposable on $V \times W$ that respects the relations given by multilinear maps. For example, we know for any bilinear $f$ that $$ f(\lambda v, w) = \lambda f(v,w) \\ f(v_1+v_2, w) = f(v_1, w) + f(v_2, w) $$ and similarly for the second slot. One might think to take the quotient $X = V \otimes W := V \times W / S$ where $$S = span(\{\lambda (v, w) - (\lambda v, w) \} \bigcup \{\lambda (v, w) - (v, \lambda w) \} \\ \bigcup \{(v_1 + v_2, w) - (v_1, w) - (v_2, w) \} \bigcup \{ (v, w_1+w_2) - (v, w_1) - (v, w_2) \})$$ Then on lets say vectors of the first form $$ T_f(\lambda [(v, w)]) = \lambda f(v, w) = T([(\lambda v, w)]) = f(\lambda v, w)$$ Unfortunately, this doesn't work, since we simultaneously have, by the vector space operations on quotients $$ T_f(\lambda [(v, w)]) = T_f([\lambda (v, w)]) = T_f([(\lambda v, \lambda w)]) = f(\lambda v, \lambda w) = \lambda^2 f(v, w) $$ which is a contradiction if $\lambda \neq 1$.

An even more ambitious approach

The student now thinks "perhaps we've quotiented out too much" and ops to do something bold: He forgets the structure on $V \times W$ by using the forgetful functor $\mathscr{U}$ and now has a set of pairs $$ S = \mathscr{U}(V \times W) = \{(v,w) | v \in V, w \in W\} $$ with no vector space structure. Then he figures he $\it may$ need to use a quotient later, so makes a vector space from $S$ as follows: $$X = ((S/\sim), \oplus, \odot)$$ This will always be possible to make into a vector space as long as the cardinality of $S/\sim$ is $\mathfrak{c}$, so why not. Now all that remains is to show that there are such $\sim, \oplus, \odot$ that makes $X$ the product $V \otimes W$ we want, or show that when assuming there are such, a contradiction arises.

Unfortunately, a contradiction arises.

Assume for every bilinear map $f: V \times W \to Y$, $\exists !T_f: X \to Y : T_f([v,w]) = f(v,w)$. Even though we've relaxed the definition of $X$ so much that now there is a relation and 2 operations we have the luxury to define however we want, it's still not enough to generate linear maps from bilinear ones. To see this, recall that the image of any linear map must be a subspace of its codomain. This is not the case for bilinear maps. As an example, take the bilinear map $$ f: \reals^n \times \reals^n \to \mathcal{M}_{n \times n} \\ v, w \mapsto vw^T $$ That is, $f$ sends vectors in $\reals^n, n > 1$ to matrices via the outer product. But notice for any $v, w$ $$ f(v,w) = vw^T = \begin{bmatrix} | & \ldots & | \\ w_1 v & \ldots & w_n v \\ | & \ldots & | \end{bmatrix} $$ This is clearly rank 1, all the columns are linearly dependent! The situation worsens, suppose $v, w = e_1, e_1$, then $$f(e_1, e_1) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & \ldots \\ 0 & 0 & \\ \vdots & & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$ but also $$ f(e_2, e_2) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & \ldots \\ 0 & 1 & \\ \vdots & & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$ so adding them together gives a rank 2 matrix! More generally, for $i,j,k,l$ where $i \neq k, j\neq l$, $f(e_i, e_j) + f(e_k, e_l)$ is going to give a rank 2 matrix. So this means $Im(T_f) \subseteq I= \{M \in \mathcal{M}_{n \times n} | rank(M) \leq 1\}$ but linear combinations of $M \in Im(T_f)$ lie outside of $I$, so $Im(T_f)$ is clearly not a vector subspace. Thus $T_f$ isn't a linear map, contradicting our claim.

Something larger

All is not lost, the student has gained $\mathscr{wisdom}$. He realizes that the key issue with his *even more ambitious approach* is that elements in $X$ are, even though the quotient is a set quotient and not a vector space quotient, still just elements of the form $[v,w]$. Sure they may be equivalence classes of a more flexible nature, but if every linear combination of vectors collapses to a single element, then the image of $T_f$ on that vector is still going to be rank 1, for the example $f$ from earlier. It would be nice if they didn't just collapse to a single element, because then, we could say $$ T_f([(e_1, e_1)] + [(e_2, e_2)] \neq T([(v, w)])$$ for any $[(v, w)] \in X$. That is to say, the only way to calculate the above is to use lineary of $T_f$ to split it to the sum on both vectors. This avoids the contradiction where the image on that sum is both rank 1 and rank 2. This is exactly what a $ \text{\bf formal sum/linear combination}$ does. It gives you sums that are irreduceable to single elements. Many authors will define the formal combination by simply stating "let $\sum a_i [v_i, w_i] $ be a formal linear combination where $v_i, w_i \in V \times W$". That is, they define these new objects $[v_i, w_i]$ in a $\text{\bf free space}$, where elements dont reduce when they are added or multiplied against a scalar. Do not mistake me, they do ${\it add/scale}$, it's just that the "pure" single elements dont reduce with other pure elements and scalars into another pure element. You may also see $\varepsilon_{v,w}$ instead of $[v,w]$ which I prefer, since it doesn't conflict with the equivalence class representative notation. Formally, let $\mathcal{B}$ be the formal symbol set over the underlying set $V \times W$ $$ \mathcal{B} = \{\varepsilon_{v, w} | v \in V, w \in W\} $$ As a reminder, this is just a set of independent elements, there is no structure yet and no way to combine them. Now let $F$ be the free space spanned by $\mathcal{B}$ $$ F = span(\mathcal{B}) $$ where addition and scalar multiplication are given as "formal" operations, that is $$c \varepsilon_{v, w} \\ \varepsilon_{v_1, w_1} + \varepsilon_{v_2, w_2} $$ dont reduce. Yea, I don't like this either, but it's what you'll often see/hear people say. Let's actually be formal. It's not even slightly difficult to formalize this because we have another domain where addition and scalar multiplication are defined, the function domain.

Intermezzo: The Free Space

Let $F^{V \times W}$ be the functional space over $V \times W$, that is $$ F^{V \times W} = \{f: V \times W \to \reals \} $$ There's a special subset $$ \mathcal{B} = \{ \varepsilon_{v,w}: (p,q)\mapsto \delta_{p,v}\delta_{q,w}\} \subset F^{V \times W} $$ where $\delta_{-,-}$ is the dirac delta. So these $\varepsilon$ just pick out each element in $V \times W$, kind of like the $\varepsilon$ from earlier. Now let $$ F^{(V \times W)} = span(\mathcal{B}) $$ where addition and scalar multiplication are as you'd expect for functions: pointwise. They aren't some formal nonesense anymore (unless you consider the entirety of math formal nonsense). What we have now is identical (up to isomorphism) to the free space from earlier, and this is also called the free space over $V \times W$. Importantly, while these functions do combine to functions in $F^{(V \times W)}$, they absolutely do NOT combine to functions in $\mathcal{B}$, since no function in $\mathcal{B}$ picks out more than one $(v, w)$. Thus, if we want to use $\varepsilon$ to represent the sum of let's say $\varepsilon_{v_1, w_1} + c\varepsilon_{v_2, w_2}$, we have to leave it as just that, there is no such $\varepsilon_{v_3, w_3}$ giving the required sum. This is exactly the syntax we need to "build something larger".
Okay we have a free space, and I hope we agree that the one given in the intermezzo is ideal. $\it Now$ we can quotient out a set defined by the relations given by bilinear maps. That is, let $$ S = span(\{\lambda \varepsilon_{v, w} - \varepsilon_{\lambda v, w} \} \bigcup \{\lambda \varepsilon_{v, w} - \varepsilon_{ v, \lambda w} \} \\ \bigcup \{\varepsilon_{v_1 + v_2, w} - \varepsilon_{v_1, w} - \varepsilon_{v_2, w} \} \bigcup \{\varepsilon_{v, w_1 + w_2} - \varepsilon_{v, w_1} - \varepsilon_{v, w_2} \}) \subset F^{(V \times W)} $$ and define, for one last time, our beloved $X$: $$X = F^{(V \times W)} / S $$ Finally, we can show that this space is large enough so that bilinear maps can be represented as linear maps without conflicts in the image. A word on notation: elements of $X = V \otimes W$ are $[\sum c_i e_{v_i, w_i}]$ for a finite number of nonzero $c_i$. Denote $$ v \otimes w = [e_{v, w}] $$ so if ${\bf x} \in V \otimes W $ then $$ {\bf x} = [\sum c_i e_{v_i, w_i}] = \sum c_i [e_{v_i, w_i}] = \sum c_i v_i \otimes w_i $$Let $f: V \times W \to Y$ be an arbitrary bilinear map. Define $$T_f(v \otimes w) = f(v, w) $$ but now, since our space contains many linearly independent $v_i \otimes w_i = [\varepsilon_{v_i, w_i}]$ that obviously can't reduce, we are going to need a way to define $T_f$ on the entire $V \otimes W$, not just on "pure" elements $v \otimes w$. There is actually only one thing we can do; linearly extend: $$ T_f(\sum c_i v_i \otimes w_i) = \sum c_i T_f(v_i \otimes w_i) $$ which is enforced by linearity regardless. So, $T_f$ is linear by construction. And also by construction it agrees with our bilinear map $f$ on the domain of $f$ (from the perspective of the linear function, on pure elements). We don't need to check that the image is a subspace, or be nervous about any other contradictions, since earlier we didn't construct a linear function from a bilinear one, we assumed it existed, but here the construction is explicit.
